## Figure 1. How much of compromise risk is topology?

File: `fig1_variance_split.pdf`

Share of the variation in compromise attributable to topology (which network) and to configuration (which software runs where), estimated as variance components on the logit scale within each density level, so the two shares sum to 100%. Overall, topology explains 26% (p = 2e-17) and configuration 74%. Topology's share falls from 38% when 20% of nodes are vulnerable to 13% at 80%: it matters most when vulnerable nodes are scarce. Decay reachability, shared exploits, floor 0.05, ceiling 0.95; 5 configurations x 2,000 simulated attacks per network and density.

**How to read it.** Blue segment: topology. Orange segment: configuration. Top bar (bold): all densities pooled; the four bars below: each density level on its own.

## Figure 3. Percolation anchor

File: `fig3_percolation.pdf`

Mean compromise of each network against (a) the share of nodes vulnerable and (b) that share divided by the network's Molloy-Reed site-percolation threshold pc = <k>/(<k^2> - <k>). If the degree distribution governs spread, the curves should line up in (b). They largely do: below the threshold (d/pc < 1) vulnerable nodes form isolated islands and compromise stays low; above it, it rises steeply. The spread that remains at a given d/pc is structure pc does not capture, plus config-to-config noise. Decay reachability, shared exploits, floor 0.05, ceiling 0.95; 5 configurations x 2,000 simulated attacks per network and density.

**How to read it.** One line per network, one point per density (20/40/60/80%). Line colour: network size, lighter = smaller (colour scale on the right). Dashed vertical line in (b): the threshold, d = pc.

## Figure 4. Networks with a lower percolation threshold are compromised more

File: `fig4_risk_vs_threshold.pdf`

Mean compromise of each network, averaged over all densities and configurations, against its percolation threshold pc, the share of nodes that must be vulnerable for a giant connected cluster to form. Spearman rho = -0.95 (p = 1e-10, n = 20). pc depends only on the degree distribution, so this is a structural mechanism rather than a size effect (see Figure 5).

**How to read it.** One point per network, named. Dashed line: least-squares fit of risk on log pc.

## Figure 5. Risk does not depend on network size

File: `fig5_size.pdf`

Mean compromise of each network against its node count. Spearman rho = +0.27 (p = 0.26, n = 20): no significant size effect, so the structural signal in Figures 3-4 is not network size in disguise.

**How to read it.** One point per network, named. Dashed curve: 1/n, the share compromised if the attack never leaves the entry node; the gap between a point and the curve is spread beyond the entry.

## Figure 6. What makes one configuration riskier than another

File: `fig6_config_severity.pdf`

Each point is one configuration, compared with the other configurations of the same network at the same density, so topology and density are held fixed. A configuration whose nodes are on average more exploitable is compromised more: r = +0.91 (r = +0.86 on the logit scale used in the variance split, i.e. 74% of the configuration effect). The mean is driven by how many vulnerable nodes run the famous vulnerable versions (p 0.77-0.95) rather than the patched ones (p 0.05-0.63). Where the attacker enters matters little: the entry node's software and degree each correlate below 0.15 with this residual risk (one-off check, 2026-10-13). Each point: 2,000 simulated attacks.

**How to read it.** One point per configuration (400 in all, every network and density). Horizontal: how much more (right) or less (left) exploitable the configuration is than its siblings. Vertical: how much more or less of the network it loses, in percentage points. Dashed line: least-squares fit (slope 1.2 pp per 0.01 of mean p). Thin lines: zero on both axes.

## Figure 7. Rewiring with degrees fixed keeps most of each network's risk

File: `fig7_rewiring.pdf`

Each network's mean compromise on its real graph and on 20 randomised copies that keep every node's degree (and so pc) but scramble which nodes are linked. Configurations are held fixed. The ranking of networks largely survives (Spearman +0.81) and the average changes by +1.3 points, so the degree distribution carries most of topology's effect. Where risk does change, it follows path length: networks whose paths shorten when rewired get riskier (Spearman -0.94 between change in risk and change in normalised path length). The largest change is Colt (32% to 45%), whose long geographic chains gain shortcuts.

**How to read it.** One row per network, sorted by risk on the real graph. Grey dot: real graph. Blue dot: mean of the 20 rewired copies. Grey bar: the change between them.
